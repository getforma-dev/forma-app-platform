For the author, not the reader. Derived from `design.md` as changed after the phase 9 reception (the lead fills the delivery tag) and from `design-demo.md`; every number below is in the full document, except the page counts and the record's page numbers, which are the build's: `output/build.json` of 2026-10-23, 45 pages, with the diagram pages the lead read from that build, to be re-read from `npm run pages` before delivery. Sections in brackets are the record's; "platform N" is a section of the platform record. The audience is a YC partner watching the six-act recording in a sitting of about twenty minutes with the demo's edition beside it, and the engineer who builds the demo. Present it as a recording of a proposed design on a measured base: Forma 3.0.0 and fAuth are built at their commits, and the platform's review rendered a prototype canvas of four kinds; nothing of the demo's own is built yet; the data is synthetic; the recording joins one kept take per act; every seeded, simulated or manual step is captioned on screen as it happens, logged, and excluded from S07's acceptance measures. ## The pitch

- Thirty seconds: Northstar is a fictional company on Forma, a company app platform. In six acts Maya turns two small files into an investigation app by asking for it, Sarah reviews and approves it, and Jordan reuses her work and starts his own. Then Maya makes a finance review queue the same way, on the same build. Every number has a receipt, a second organization replays our calls and gets nothing, and the proof ships in a packet.
- Two minutes: Start with what a fake would look like, because each shortcut is invisible in a video: two fixed dashboards; a handler that divides by ten minutes in code, so a change to the denominator is a commit the recording cannot attribute; a decision kept in browser state, which a reload loses; one session playing three people, so no publish is refused and nothing is ever denied. That is why the record, not the recording, carries the evidence. Then the six acts. Maya signs in through a real tenant boundary, the organization taken from a signed token, and lands on the company home: Security and Finance, no app yet. She drops two files, which a sandbox with no network and no keys parses into immutable revisions. She asks in words; three views come back from the index, the rest from one model call, a typed definition the platform admits whole or refuses whole, and the denominator arrives as a patch with a readable diff. Her own approval is refused; Sarah approves; 1,140,000 bytes come back with receipts; we restart the process, and the target is the case back within 30 s. Jordan reopens her case by its id, imports his own file and starts his own work, and when Maya unshares from the share control he loses her case within 5 s. Then Spend Review, by the same path on the same build: Sarah approves it in ten seconds, a decision survives a lost response and refuses a stale edit, and a canary replay gets 404 on camera. Close on the honest part: one kept take per act; every staged step captioned on screen as it happens; one unscripted take a rehearsal; one host and one operator; a client row added by hand; and the spike has not run yet. The first thing we do is the spike.

## Per chapter

### What is real, what is labeled, and how the recording is cut

- Three points to make: every scene runs the platform's code on one build, every number with a receipt; every seeded, simulated or manual step is captioned on screen as it happens, logged and excluded from S07's acceptance measures, the simulated ones made by the rehearsal script at the client while the server's answers are real; the recording joins one kept take per act, each starting from the state the last ended in, so Sarah approves what act 3 formed.
- The number to say: twenty whole-run takes at most, ten rehearsals filmed at most twice; one labeled unscripted take a rehearsal; the saved states `s0` and `s2` to `s5`.
- The trade-off to defend: per-act takes from saved states, so retaking act N restarts from the state before it and retakes the acts after it; the lost response staged rather than waited for, because a duplicate submission yielding one mutation is the stronger proof (8, 13.3, 7.2).

### The system in two pictures

- Three points to make: one place decides, the workspace server, the only caller of the control plane and the data service; three ownership rules, publications and policy by the control plane, evidence and records by the data service, the model by the workers; the parts share one process but keep their own database roles, so the write grants survive, while the task role can connect as all of them, so the walls stop a logic bug and not code in the process.
- The number to say: 404 before any data for every canary replay; one data key per organization.
- The trade-off to defend: one process on one host, one failure domain, against the platform's roles as separate deployments, which would give one founder eight things to restart during a take (12.2, D1).

### Act 1, entering the workspace

- Three points to make: fAuth issues a signed token, `tenant_id` the organization and `sub` the person, and a token without `tenant_id` mints nothing; the company home comes before any app, the Security and Finance spaces with creating and resuming as its choices, a click and `/security` one space id, recents listing permitted items only; the session is a PostgreSQL row, so a restart is a reload, and the take starts from `s0`, seeded, in a profile signed out of both the demo and fAuth.
- The number to say: ten quiet seconds of sign-in; fAuth's 44 smoke tests pass at its commit; sessions live 12 hours, idle 1 hour.
- The trade-off to defend: a second deployment and a client row by hand, against an organization dropdown that is not a reduction; the platform's minimal login behind the same adapter is the fallback, proven on every build and switched before a take, never during (12.2, 11.1).

### Act 2, bringing evidence

- Three points to make: the parser is a WebAssembly module with no system interface, so no socket and no path to credentials; revision 1 of each file is immutable, under Northstar's own key; Maya confirms bytes per event, UTC, the half-open window [10:00, 10:10) and the match rule, an exact inventory entry, else the most specific range, unmatched meaning absent from this inventory, not malicious.
- The number to say: 6 rows and 0 rejected, all six of `build-worker-17`, then 2 and 0; the 4 MiB spreadsheet that inflates past 50 MiB, refused on camera, with the process alive.
- The trade-off to defend: caps below the platform's, a tenth for CSV and half for spreadsheets, a reduction whose seam is the same pipeline; the sandbox inside the one process, where an escape is rated low in likelihood but would reach every role, accepted with the one process (6.4, appendix C, 10.7).

### Act 3, forming and transforming

- Three points to make: retrieval first, from Northstar's index of the catalog's metadata and curated examples, so three views are retrieved, the network-range rule adapted and the rest generated, each view marked; one model call with eight synthetic rows, its proposal data that admission checks whole against the registry; two patches by controls, not the model, revisions 2 and 3 with semantic diffs, undo 4, reapply 5, a stale patch 412.
- The number to say: 114,000 bytes a minute over the whole ten-minute window; two 60 s attempts in a five-minute act; eight synthetic rows to the provider, twenty at most.
- The trade-off to defend: a closed vocabulary, where a kind asked for on the spot is a release, not a prompt (12.1); the live model on camera, so a provider failure ends the take within 1 s and it is retaken, never continued on the fixture (6.4); the "export every secret" field is a delimited data value that can still steer the proposal, and admission against the closed vocabulary holds (7.1, appendix C).

### Act 4, publishing and running

- Three points to make: Maya's own approval is refused with 403 naming the rule, under Northstar's independent-review policy; Sarah approves the digest, the control plane alone writes publication 1, and a later patch voids the approval; every number has a receipt, and the case is pinned to publication 1 until explicit adoption.
- The number to say: 1,140,000 bytes over five of `build-worker-17`'s six events in the window, 240,000 matched, 900,000 unmatched, all of it to `198.51.100.7`; 114,000 a minute; the target of the case back within 30 s of a restart, the maximum over twenty whole-run takes reported.
- The trade-off to defend: one host and no standby, so a restart is a scene; the 30 s target holds for an in-place restart by the supervisor, and a full task replacement is unmeasured against it (5.2, 13.1). The late epoch-11 answer is the rehearsal script's, captioned simulated; the restart is the operator's, captioned manual.

### Act 5, sharing and reusing

- Three points to make: access to a case is a grant separate from access to the app, and a bare link is 404; Jordan imports his own two-row CSV for `ops-runner-04` by act 2's path, reading the inventory under his source grant, and his work has its own id and empty findings while Maya's 1,140,000 stand; Maya revokes his grant from the share control, he stays a member of Security, and every request re-reads the membership epoch, so his next refresh of her case is denied within 5 s, a still-queued query included.
- The number to say: 210,000 bytes, 30,000 unmatched, 21,000 a minute for `ops-runner-04`; 5 s.
- The trade-off to defend: no coediting, the conflict on camera being act 6's stale edit, with the expected version as the seam; the revocation is Maya's own, from the share control, and the check that enforces it is the platform's, not a demo switch (7.1, 6.1, 6.3).

### Act 6, a different app

- Three points to make: home lists the first app with its owner and publication 1 and, at the end, both apps with owners and versions; Spend Review is made on camera through act 3's interface, from primitives Northstar never used, on one build digest, with no code change and no deploy between acts 3 and 6, and Sarah approves it under the same per-organization policy, in ten seconds; each decision is one transaction, version 1 under `ik-1`, the forced retry answered by the stored outcome before any version check, the stale edit under `ik-3` refused with 409, version 2 under `ik-2`, and one canary replay gets 404 on camera.
- The number to say: 64,000 cents over three expenses; the queue at 20,000; EXP-002 at 45,000; two transactions, two record versions, two audit events.
- The trade-off to defend: the dropped response, the stale and reused-key requests and the late Finance answer are the rehearsal script's at the client, captioned simulated, the server's answers real; the drop is staged because a duplicate submission yielding one mutation is the stronger proof (7.2). If asked about `ik-3`: the keys are named by what commits, `ik-1` and `ik-2` the two commands that do.

### What the demo is judged on, and what it does not prove

- Three points to make: five requirements, ranked: generality, zero commits and deploys between acts 3 and 6, the claim the recording is judged on; security, never traded; then accuracy, consistency and the restart; it proves mechanisms on synthetic data at one team's scale, not adoption, scale or availability; eight reductions each name the platform mechanism that replaces them, and four shortcuts are refused, a login dropdown, work only in browser memory, faked record commits and a hardcoded act 6.
- The number to say: five ranked requirements (N9, N7, N6, N5, N2); eight reductions; four refused shortcuts.
- The trade-off to defend: the model is shown only on the fixtures' eight synthetic rows, past which it is unknown; the one labeled unscripted take a rehearsal is the test beyond them (13.1, 13.2).

### How it grows into the platform, and what we do not know yet

- Three points to make: three of the platform's five migration steps are true at birth, because the organization's id is in every key from the first row (11.1, D2); what we considered instead, from doing nothing to a no-code tool, and the three one-way doors, D2, D3 and D7, the first two binding the platform's four; the spike runs first, and its first number is whether the route holds all nine kinds.
- The number to say: 27,674 of 28,100 bytes with four kinds; the 426 left are Forma's own share, spent only by a kind that imports a Forma module the four did not; a kind's own code counts against the route's 365 KiB; ten rehearsals, zero platform-caused retakes in the last three.
- The trade-off to defend: one ECS task by default so the platform's deployment carries over, kept only if a timed task replacement meets 30 s, else one virtual machine (D10, 13.2).

### Security and operations in brief, and the accepted risks

- Three points to make: two walls and a test keep the organizations apart, row-level security and a data key per organization behind the canary suite on every build and at both ends of every take, one replay on camera; one operator runs every take by the same six steps, snapshot, reset, sign-in, the check and the canary, record, the canary again and the packet; five accepted risks said plainly: no second person and no breakglass, one host and one process, every operator key on one shell, the client row by hand, the reset's route in the platform's binary.
- The number to say: 404 before any data, beside the owner's 200; about $170 a month, hosting and tokens together, $17 a rehearsal, $1.10 of it tokens; five accepted risks.
- The trade-off to defend: no second person and no breakglass, accepted because there is one founder, every operator action is listed against the script, and the data is synthetic (10.7).

### For the engineer, only if asked

- Three points to make: the spike's path on the real library and route, from definition to reopen with the duplicate submission and the version conflict; one actor at a time, so a take's burst is six operations, two running and four waiting; the operator's keys stay in the keychain and die when their job is done, and the operator's machine is off the network except while a rehearsal runs.
- The number to say: about $170 a month, hosting and tokens together; 120 generation attempts; a warm operation under about 650 ms keeps the 2 s refresh.
- The trade-off to defend: the reset's route in the platform's binary, dead without the flag and its role and proven on every build, because a second binary would break the one build digest (6.7, 10.7).

## Questions to expect, with answers

The partner's five from the reader check come first, in the order a partner asks them; Q29 to Q33 are the reception's, added after its triage.

1. "Is the recording one take, or per-act takes from seeded states, and is the app Sarah approves the one I watched being formed?" Per-act takes, joined: one kept take per act, each starting from the saved state the last one ended in, so Sarah approves exactly what act 3 formed on camera, and retaking act N retakes the acts after it. Each take's starting state is captioned seeded on screen, logged and excluded from S07's acceptance measures (8, 13.3).
2. "The model is called live: how do the same numbers appear in every take, and how many valid but different proposals did you throw away?" The numbers are queries over the fixtures, so they repeat whatever the model's arrangement of the views; the arrangement itself may not repeat. The rehearsal log counts every discarded take, so the packet answers how many were thrown away (13.3, 4.1).
3. "In act 3, what came from the model and what was already there?" Summary, table and timeline are retrieved from Northstar's index, which at the start of act 3 holds the catalog's metadata and curated examples; the network-range rule is adapted; the rest comes from one provider call that saw eight synthetic rows. The two patches, the transfer rate and the observation form, are made by controls, not by the model, and each view says which of the three it is (7.1; platform D7, R4).
4. "What happens if I type my own request, or ask for something the vocabulary lacks, and will even the nine kinds fit?" Inside the vocabulary a request takes the same path; outside, admission fails it whole, stores nothing partial and names the missing kind, because a new kind is a release, not a prompt. Each rehearsal films one labeled unscripted take of exactly this, a third app named by someone outside the team, filed admitted or refused naming the missing kind (13.2; platform R4). Whether all nine kinds fit is the spike's first number: four compiled at 27,674 of 28,100 bytes, and the 426 left are Forma's own share, spent only by a kind that imports a Forma module the four did not, while a kind's own code counts against the route's 365 KiB (12.1, 6.5).
5. "Who is at the keyboard, and what is scripted?" I am, as the one operator, on one machine: Maya, Sarah and Jordan are three accounts in three Chrome profiles, and the rehearsal script brings one forward at a time over the Chrome DevTools Protocol and makes the captioned faults at the client, adding no code to the product. The independent review is a different account's approval, which the policy checks; the canary suite is scripted too, with one replay on camera in act 6 and the suite's results in the packet (appendix A, 11.1, 13.2).
6. "Is the second app made on camera or loaded?" Made: Maya forms Spend Review through act 3's interface from primitives Northstar never used, on the same build digest, with no code change and no deploy between acts 3 and 6. The evidence recorder reads the deploy log between the acts and fails acceptance on any deploy; act 4's restart on the same image digest is not one (13.3, 4.2, 6.7).
7. "What in this recording is simulated, and how would I know while watching?" Every staged step is captioned on screen as it happens, logged in the rehearsal log and excluded from S07's acceptance measures. Seeded: each take's starting state and the spike's fixture proposal, which never appears in a take; simulated, by the rehearsal script at the client with the server's answers real: act 4's late epoch-11 answer, act 6's dropped response, its stale and reused-key requests and its late Finance answer; manual: only act 4's restart, by the operator (13.3, 3.1, appendix A).
8. "Why not build it on a no-code tool?" The record's reuse-or-buy row rejects a no-code tool, a framework owning the control flow, because it runs generated code as trusted; a Forma app is a typed definition the platform validates and renders with first-party components, so what runs is the definition Sarah approved (12.2, K1). If the partner names a product, answer for the class: the record names none, and the claims check verified none.
9. "Does any of this survive the demo?" Every reduction names the platform mechanism that replaces it, and the demo runs the platform's code with no fork, on its stores from the first row, so three of the platform's five migration steps are true at birth. What does not survive is the rehearsal tooling: before any real organization the flag goes off, the reset's role is dropped, the saved states are deleted and the keys are shredded (6.8, 11.1).
10. "Why your own identity service?" Because the canary's denial and Jordan's revocation then rest on a real tenant boundary, the organization taken from a signed token, and no password reaches the platform. The demo binds only fAuth's token contract behind one adapter, with the platform's minimal login as a fallback proven on every build; the cost is a second deployment for one act and a client row by hand (12.2, 11.1).
11. "What breaks if the model is wrong?" A proposal outside the vocabulary fails admission whole and nothing partial is stored; a valid but wrong one meets the semantic diff, the preview and a reviewer who is not its author, and a failing draft never disables the published version. If the provider is down, generation fails visibly within 1 s while navigation, queries and forms keep working, and the take is retaken, never continued on the fixture (7.1, 4.1, 4.2, 6.4).
12. "What does a number's receipt prove?" It is the server's record of exactly which sources, revisions, parameters and policy produced the number, and a saved view's receipt also says which record version it saw. The evidence recorder checks every number shown against its receipt's result digest, and one mismatch fails the take (the reading note, 7.2, 6.7, 11.3).
13. "How do you keep two organizations apart?" The organization's id leads every key from the first row and comes only from the signed token; every table is under row-level security, and each organization's files are under its own data key, so the canary's key never unwraps Northstar's bytes. The canary replays every call with Northstar's identifiers on every build and at both ends of every take, and any answer but 404, or a replay without the owner's 200 in the same take, fails the take (10.4, 8, 12.2).
14. "What did the spike measure?" Nothing yet, and say so first: the spike runs before any rehearsal and will measure the route's bytes with all nine kinds against 28,100, the commit, the restart's maximum and one bounded spreadsheet refusal. What has been measured is the platform review's composition fixture, four kinds at 27,674 of 28,100 bytes, leaving 426 of Forma's own share (5, 5.2, 6.5, 13.1).
15. "What does a rehearsal cost?" About $17 at ten rehearsals a month, $1.10 of it tokens, out of about $170 a month, hosting and tokens together, the hosting running whether or not we rehearse; the prices are assumed US East list, a European region about a tenth more, with fAuth's hosting outside. The real month is unknown until the meter reports it with the recording, and the provider key carries a spend limit just above the month's tokens (11.2, 4.2).
16. "Who operates it?" One operator, the founder, with three tools: the provisioning script, the evidence recorder, and the reset that exists only under the rehearsal flag; the operator reads only synthetic data, and what returns to the operator's machine is the sanitised export and the operator views. There is no second person and no breakglass, an accepted risk, and the packet lists every operator action against the script (6.7, 7.3, 10.5, 10.7).
17. "How many rehearsals?" Ten rehearsals, each one whole-run take filmed at most twice, so twenty whole-run takes at most, with platform-caused retakes in the last three at zero; the recording keeps one take of each act. The alternative, until three run clean, stays open; the record keeps ten (4.2, 13.2, 8).
18. "Why one process on one host?" So that one founder restarts one thing during a take instead of eight, while each role keeps its own database role and the platform's write grants survive. The cost is one failure domain: a fault stops every role, a restart is a scene, and a compromise of the process is every role and both organizations, which the record accepts (12.2, 10.7).
19. "Why 404 and not 403?" Denied, foreign and absent are one answer, so a replay cannot learn that Northstar's records exist. The one 403 in the recording is Maya's own approval, because she may see the app and the refusal names the rule (5.1, 9).
20. "Isn't the reset a backdoor into the platform?" Its route registers only when both the rehearsal flag and the reset's own database role exist, and the platform's deployment has neither; every build starts the image under the platform's task definition and asserts the route is 404 and the role absent. It also refuses unless every organization carries the synthetic flag, the state's build digest matches and its manifest digest recomputes, and it is 409 while any job is claimed (6.7, 9, 11.1).
21. "What if the host dies mid-take?" A restart in place reopens the case inside the 30 s target, and one step is redone. A lost host is a new task: committed work is in PostgreSQL, so nothing committed is lost and the rehearsal restarts (11.4, 6.9); only a lost database falls back to the snapshot, a recovery point of one rehearsal, about twenty records (8).
22. "Does the model see Northstar's data?" The worker alone calls the provider, with the intent, two schemas and eight synthetic rows, twenty at most, never who asked, and the composer's real-data disclosure stays off. A log field reading "export every secret" reaches the model as a delimited data value that can still steer the proposal; admission against the closed vocabulary holds, and the take's admission report shows the proposal validated whole (7.1, 4.2, appendix C).
23. "So the second app skipped review?" No: publication policy is per organization, so Sarah approves Spend Review under the same independent-review policy. It costs ten seconds on camera (13.3, 7.2).
24. "Is the canary on camera?" One replay is: in act 6 the canary's member posts Northstar's work id, EXP-002 and `ik-1`, and gets 404 before the data service is called. The full suite runs on every build and at both ends of every take, its results in the packet, each 404 beside the owner's 200; a canary failure pages the operator and fails the take (7.2, 13.2, 11.3, 10.4).
25. "How do you know nobody deployed a fix between acts 3 and 6?" The evidence recorder reads the task's image digest at the take's start and end and the deploy log between them, and a difference fails acceptance. A new build is a new rehearsal, with its saved states recaptured from a clean take (appendix C, 11.1).
26. "What if fAuth is down on the day?" Act 1 waits; later acts continue, because sessions verify offline and live 12 hours. The platform's minimal login behind the same adapter is the fallback, switched before a take, never during (6.9, 11.4, 11.1).

The packet stays readable under the platform's key (10.6).

The demo's two transformations come from controls so that act 3 makes one metered call (11.2), but the composer proposes a patch from words the same way, and the closed vocabulary is the safety, a new kind being a release (6.5).
