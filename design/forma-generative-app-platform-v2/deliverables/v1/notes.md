# Presenter notes: forma-generative-app-platform-v2, investor edition

For the author, not the reader. Derived from `design.md` at the phase 7 gate commit (the lead fills the delivery tag) and from `design-investor.md`; every number below is in the full document, except the page counts under the gaps, which are the build's (`output/build.json`), and the sections in brackets are the record's. The audience is the founder presenting and a technical investor, a YC-style partner with an engineering background, reading the edition alone in about an hour before a partner meeting. Present it as a design with its measured base named: the rendering is built on Forma 3.0.0 as shipped and fAuth is built at its pin; everything else is proposed; the data is synthetic; there is no customer.

## The pitch

- Thirty seconds: Forma is a company app platform. A team member describes a small app over the company's own imported data, a model proposes it as data rather than code, a colleague reviews and publishes it, and the team runs it with every number carrying a receipt that says exactly which files, versions and parameters produced it. It replaces the spreadsheet where the denominator sits in a cell, the rule lives in someone's head, and the pasted rows leave the company. A new app is a database row, not a deployment, and pages render in Rust with no JavaScript engine on the server. It runs on my own open-source framework, Forma 3.0.0, released 2026-10-20. Ten organizations cost about $1,150 a month. The proof is two different apps, a security investigation and a finance queue, through one path on one build with no deploy between them.
- Two minutes: Start with Maya. She exports ten minutes of connection events to a CSV, matches destinations with a lookup that takes the first hit so one connection can count twice, sums 1,140,000 bytes and divides by five minutes when she meant ten, pastes the rows into a chat tab that leaves the company and forgets the answer when it closes, and emails a screenshot; her colleague does it all again. Finance does the same with money, and the decision is an overwritable cell. Forma is the mechanism that replaces each of those. The model proposes a typed definition, views from a closed vocabulary, a bounded query grammar, record schemas and declared commands; it never writes code, markup or a grant, so what the reviewer approves is what runs, and the diff she approves is exact. Every number is computed on the server over an immutable copy of the source with a receipt; every decision written back is one database transaction keyed so a retry returns the same outcome and a stale edit gets a conflict, never a silent overwrite. Ten organizations share one pool: the organization's id leads every key, the database filters every table, each organization has its own encryption key, and a canary organization replays every call with the real one's identifiers on every release and must get nothing. One person runs it: one binary in four roles on managed services, a three-wave rollout that one metric alone rolls back. Close on the honest part: the rendering is prototyped, the rest is a design; the browser route has 426 bytes of headroom; the cold aggregate over a million rows is unmeasured; the sign-in service's key sits with me; there is no customer. The first step is a spike that runs the whole path and measures the two numbers.

## Per chapter

### Summary in plain words

- Three points to make: an app is data the platform validates, not code it trusts; every number has a receipt and every decision is one transaction; ten organizations share one pool with two independent walls between them.
- The number to say: ten organizations, 100 seats, 60 query operations a second at peak, about $1,150 a month.
- The trade-off to defend: isolation and generality over expressiveness and generation latency, a closed vocabulary in which a new widget is a release, and every proposal a background job (record 1, 5).

### 1. What it is and why now

- Three points to make: Forma 3.0.0 withholds identity, storage, jobs and tenancy by design so they are designed once, here; the three prototypes fail for stated reasons in their code, one person on loopback, a registry that loads once, a 2,000-row dataset; design A was security-first with model-written markup, and this record generalises it on a typed definition.
- The number to say: 1,140,000 bytes, 228,000 a minute over five minutes against 114,000 over ten; the sheet shows the number without its denominator.
- The trade-off to defend: an industry-agnostic platform rather than a security product; the controlling definition governs where the two product definitions differ, and the YC request prescribes no app and promises no funding (record 2, A13).

### 2. How it works: the system in two pictures

- Three points to make: one place that decides, the workspace server, which the control plane and the data service re-check, and the roles behind it hold no member session; three ownership rules, publications by the control plane alone, evidence and records by the data service alone, the provider reached by workers alone; the fit check puts 6 operations in flight at peak on one data node with a cap of 8, 2 per organization.
- The number to say: 60 operations a second at peak is 3.3 times the average; a six-view refresh runs as three waves of two, about 300 ms warm at an assumed 100 ms an operation.
- The trade-off to defend: eight containers for one founder, because each role boundary is a credential that cannot write what another writes; the alternative, a service per concern with its own store, makes a publication a distributed transaction (record 5.2, 12.2).

### 3. Why the second app is the proof

- Three points to make: G1 is done when both apps come through one authoring path on one build digest with no platform code branching on a tenant or space name; the grammar has no URL, path, identifier or grant type, so admission is whole-output validation plus limits of 24 views, 8 operations a view, 256 KiB and 500 rows a page; every creation is labelled retrieved, adapted or generated, and of ten supported and five adversarial intents every unsupported one abstains naming the missing capability.
- The number to say: 27,674 of 28,100 bytes compressed with four kinds, 426 bytes of headroom; design A's markup runtime alone is 28,337.
- The trade-off to defend: the closed vocabulary. The demo shows assembly, not a bespoke widget; the custom-widget tier is exactly design A's path and waits for a customer to ask (record 12.1, 13.1 RK6).

### 4. What keeps organizations apart

- Three points to make: two independent walls, the id from the verified token plus row-level security against a forgotten scope, the adapter as the id's only source plus the canary suite against a wrong id; the model sees schemas and 20 synthetic rows unless a policy names fields, has five tools that return no rows, and the member submits every change; the operator sees no organization data without a one-hour grant the organization's administrator approves.
- The number to say: revocation reaches an open display within 5 seconds; a denial is 404 so existence does not leak; eleven accepted risks, all the head's.
- The trade-off to defend: pooled tenancy over a silo. A silo is ten databases now and a hundred at 10x run by one operator; the pool's price is a suite that runs on every release because one policy bug is every organization's bug (record 12.2 D3, 10.7 AR2).

### 5. What a number means

- Three points to make: a receipt pins the definition digest, operator versions, revision digests, record snapshot, parameters and policy epoch, and the result cache is keyed by it so it can never be stale; evidence never changes and records always do, hence immutable Parquet under DuckDB for sources and PostgreSQL for records; a record command is one transaction in a fixed lock order, retry row first so a duplicate returns the stored outcome before any version check.
- The number to say: one hot record sustains about 50 commits a second at an assumed 20 ms commit, against 2 a second tested; a query is interrupted at 1.8 seconds and answers "narrow the query", never a partial number.
- The trade-off to defend: two engines and an unmeasured cold aggregate. The fallback, aggregates computed at publication, changes DuckDB's plan and not the grammar, which is why D4 is one-way and DuckDB is not (record 12.2 D4, 5.2).

### 6. What it costs and how one founder runs it

- Three points to make: the data service is the driver, $340 of the $565 compute line, and the second data task buys availability, not load; no contractual availability figure until hosting, a support owner and failure measurements exist, so 99.5% is an internal objective; a release goes out in three waves and `forma_canary_failures_total` alone rolls one back.
- The number to say: about $1,150 a month, $115 an organization; about $4,500 at 10x, $45 an organization; 99.5% is 3.65 hours a month and one full restore at 4 hours exceeds it by 21 minutes.
- The trade-off to defend: one region, so losing it stops service until it returns because the backups stay in it (AR10); and plaintext HTTP inside the VPC because mutual TLS needs a certificate authority nobody runs (AR3) (record 11.2, 10.7).

### 7. What we do not know yet and what to do first

- Three points to make: four High risks, no customer, 426 bytes of headroom with two framework tickets assumed, fAuth's key with the founder, one founder on the release path; three questions before build, the cold aggregate inside 1.8 seconds and the route under 28,100 bytes, the provider tier, the region; four one-way doors, D1 to D4, because they are what stored apps, evidence, keys and receipts are made of.
- The number to say: the composition fixture rendered 6,752 bytes of HTML with every fixture value at first paint under the nonce CSP; that is what is built.
- The trade-off to defend: designing the doors before building, so the four are decided once before any organization stores work in them; everything else in the table reverses (record 5.3, 13).

## Questions to expect, with answers

1. "Is this a form builder with a chat box?" An app is a typed definition the platform validates, publishes under the organization's policy and runs with a receipt on every number; the chat proposes data through five tools and submits nothing. The proof is that a finance queue with a decision form and a security investigation come through one path on one build with no deploy between them (sections 5, 5.3 D1, 3.1 G1, 13.3).
2. "Why two stores and two engines?" Evidence never changes and records always do: a million-row source cannot sit in the database every role uses, and a form needs a transaction. So immutable Parquet revisions under DuckDB carry the sources and PostgreSQL carries records, work and publications, with one data service behind receipts and commands (sections 5.3 D4, 8, 12.2).
3. "How is this different from design A?" It is the same company platform, generalised beyond security, on a typed definition instead of model-written markup, which drops the sanitizer, the translator and two High/High threat rows. Public pages first, MCP Apps first and the "nothing migrates" promise were dropped as pitfalls (sections 2, 12.3, 13.3).
4. "What happens when the model is wrong?" Outside the grammar it abstains and names the missing capability; inside it, a valid but wrong proposal meets the semantic diff, the synthetic preview and a reviewer who is never the author, and every number it explains must match its receipt. In a provider outage, navigation, published queries and forms work and generation fails visibly (sections 4.1 R4, R12, R13, 6.4, 10.7 AR11).
5. "Which availability objective do you actually hold?" None contractually, until there is hosting, a support owner and failure measurements. The internal objective for the published-app path is 99.5% of valid requests a month, 3.65 hours, which the vendors' commitments compose to about 99.65% warm and 99.55% cold, and which assumes no full restore in a month because one at RTO exceeds it by 21 minutes (sections 4.2 N2, 5.2, 11.3).
6. "Has the DuckDB cold aggregate been measured?" No, and say so before the follow-up: the fit check is warm, 100 ms an operation and three waves at 300 ms, while a cold six-view refresh is three waves of up to 1.8 seconds each, which misses the 2-second target; the record carries that as its own open question, two tasks with a cap of 2 against a third task and a cap of 6, settled by load. The spike measures the cold aggregate over a 1-million-record revision first; the fallback is aggregates computed at publication, which changes the engine's plan and not the grammar or the receipts (sections 5.2, 13.1 RK5, 13.2).
7. "Does the workspace server call the control plane on every open?" No. Published apps serve from the workspace server's own reads of the publication rows, which only the control plane writes; the control plane is a soft dependency, reached for admission, approval and publication only (sections 5.2, 6.7, 7.2 step 2).
8. "Is 10x ten times the peak?" 10x is ten times the design point: 100 organizations, 1,000 seats, 300 concurrent on average and 600 operations a second at peak, where peak is every seat active, 3.3 times the average. It is met by adding tasks, eight data tasks plus one for a zone loss, with no contract or code change, confirmed by open-loop load on one build (sections 4.2 N1, A15, 11.2).
9. "What is the 5-second canvas refresh?" The canvas polls the membership epoch every 5 seconds, one read, and re-queries only on a filter change or an epoch bump; every request is reauthorized anyway, so the poll is the other half of the bound that a removed member's open display goes dark within 5 seconds (sections 6.5, 5.2, 10.2 SI4).
10. "Who authenticates the operator?" Operators sign in through fAuth as members of Forma's own operator organization, with TOTP today and a hardware key before the first real organization; the operator role is granted for a day by a second person or self-granted with a reason, audited. Reading an organization's data needs its administrator's one-hour just-in-time grant, and breakglass is a sealed role behind a hardware key whose log a named outside reviewer reads (sections 10.5, 10.7 AR1).
11. "What does fAuth cost you in focus?" The platform uses only its token contract behind one adapter, so a provider swap changes sessions, not authority; the record rates it a High risk because the live service is behind the pin, the client row is made by hand, and its signing key sits with me until a ticket moves it into KMS. The fallback is a minimal platform login behind the same adapter (sections 3.3 A11, 5.3 D6, 13.1 RK3, 10.7 AR4).
12. "How do you make money?" The record defers pricing, per seat, per organization or the meter, with the meter first, and gives the cost side only: $115 an organization at ten organizations and $45 at a hundred. The value claim, cheaper than the spreadsheet it replaces, is marked fuzzy because it can only be measured against a pilot baseline that does not exist, and no customer is the first High risk (sections 3.1 G7, 4.2 N10, 11.2, 13.1 RK1, 13.2).
13. "Why not Retool, Airtable, Glide or Claude artifacts?" Retool runs an app as code the platform trusts; Airtable puts pages over a table with no receipt behind a number; Glide keeps the sheet as the store and a decision as a cell; artifacts run generated code in a sandbox beside a conversation. None pins a typed definition to a publication record with a receipt on every number (section 12.2).
14. "What is actually built today, and nine kinds or four?" Forma 3.0.0 at its tag, which rendered four of the library's nine kinds as one canvas island at 27,674 of 28,100 bytes, Forma's own route ceiling (BUILD-819), with every fixture value at first paint; fAuth at its pin with its viability check passed; three Forma 2.x prototypes as donors. The other five kinds, among them the form, status and detail that Spend Review needs, are proposed and unmeasured on the route, as is the platform itself and every number in section 4 (sections 2, 5, 6.5, appendix B).
15. "Is the demo's generation moment staged?" No: it calls the live model with at most two attempts and fails visibly on stage if both miss, in which case the demo retrieves what the organization already has, labelled as retrieval. Only the spike, the measurement before the demo, uses a labelled fixture, and fixtures never pass the gate (sections 5.3 D8, 11.1, 13.1 RK7).
16. "What does the model provider see, and what does it keep?" Schemas, catalog metadata, the intent and 20 synthetic rows by default; real fields only under a policy the administrator writes naming the fields and the provider, and a request carrying an unnamed field is refused before egress. What a policy lets through the provider retains under its terms, an accepted risk the administrator chooses (sections 4.2 N8, 6.4, 10.7 AR7, appendix D TH19).
17. "Why pooled tenancy instead of a database per customer?" A silo is ten databases now and a hundred at 10x, each run alone by one operator, and it loses on the operating model and cost. The pool has two independent walls, the id from the verified token with row-level security, and the adapter as the id's only source with the canary suite; cells exist for an organization that outgrows the pool (sections 5.3 D3, 12.2).
18. "What about GDPR?" Every row is synthetic today, so no regime applies yet. Before any real row the first organization, as controller, needs a data protection impact assessment where the GDPR reaches it, because connection logs identify employees; the assessment fixes the data classes, the provider policy and the retention values (section 10.1).
19. "Why one binary and eight containers for one founder?" One binary is one release with one set of contracts, and the roles are deployments with their own credentials, so a role boundary is a security boundary rather than a service to run. The cost is coupling, one schema change touching every role, and the record accepts it over a service per concern, which would make a publication a distributed transaction (sections 5.3 D11, 12.2).
20. "How does a reviewer know what she approved is what runs?" Her own session approves the content digest of the admitted bytes; the control plane checks that digest against the draft's current digest, the policy version and her current membership in one SERIALIZABLE transaction, with a database constraint that she is not the author. A later patch voids the approval and the inbox renders the admitted bytes' diff and preview (sections 6.2, 10.4 TH5).
21. "Why cap an organization at 2 queries at once?" So that one organization's six-view refresh cannot take the node from the others; six warm operations at an assumed 100 ms run in three waves, about 300 ms, inside the 2-second target. The record keeps it an open question during build, two tasks and a cap of 2 against a third task and a cap of 6, settled by load (sections 5.2, 6.3, 13.2).
22. "Can a canary suite really prove isolation?" It proves the walls hold on every route, search, file, record, job and cache lookup it replays, on every release, and any hit rolls the release back; it does not prove the absence of a route it does not replay. That is why the second wall, row-level security on every table with the id forced from the session, exists independently (sections 3.1 G5, 10.4 TH1, 12.2 D3).
23. "What does the closed vocabulary cost the pitch?" The generation moment shows assembly of nine kinds, not a bespoke widget, and an app that needs a tenth waits for a release. The record's answer is that abstentions name the missing capability and the pitch leans on receipts and governance, not novelty (sections 12.1, 13.1 RK6).
24. "Who is the first customer, and will they pilot on real data?" Nobody yet, and the record says so first (RK1, 13.1: no customer, no baseline, no mitigation here). The demo is the first test; the ask after it is one named team with section 2's six steps, on real data behind a data protection impact assessment (10.1, A12), before a pilot sets any SLA (A8). (13.1, 13.2, 3.2)
25. "What is the shortest path to one organization on real data?" The demo build as it stands: one process, one host, the platform's code with no fork (demo D1 to D3), so a pilot organization is a provisioning run, not a build; what pooled tenancy, the canary suite and the four roles buy is that the second organization costs nothing to isolate and nothing to re-review, which is the claim a platform has to make before the first customer or redesign after it (D3, G5, 12.2's silo row). (5.3, 12.2, demo 6.8)
26. "Why is your own framework's byte ceiling your second-highest risk, and not a budget you raise?" Because the ceiling is the framework's promise to its users, a route whose Forma code stays under 28,100 bytes gzip, and raising it for our own canvas would be the platform eating the framework's guarantee; the 426 bytes of headroom are a measured fact at the tag (review 4.3), FORMA-232 makes the budget a build check, and the fallback is one fewer kind per route, not a bigger budget. The framework is a constraint we chose, and 13.1 rates it High because it is ours to keep. (RK2, A14, 6.5)
27. "How many hours a week, and who is the first hire?" The record gives the how, not the hours: one binary, managed stores, six paging alerts a month and the runbook rule (11.3, 11.4); it names no hire. Own it: the first hire is an operator-engineer once a pilot sets an SLA (A8), and the hours are the head's number, not the document's. (A8, 11.3)
28. "What settled the direction, insurer to security to industry-agnostic?" The product definition's own test: two apps with different purposes through one path (§18), which a security-only platform could not pass, and the review's experiment showing the typed definition renders on 3.0.0 as shipped with no framework change (review 4.3); design A's markup path waits as the custom-widget tier (12.3). (2, 12.3, review 5)
29. "What about Retool's and Airtable's own AI features?" The record positions against what those products trust at runtime, not against their assistants: an app there is code the platform trusts, or pages over a table with no receipt behind a number (12.2); their AI features change how the app is written, not what runs or what a number can prove. That is the difference to say; a feature-by-feature comparison is not in the record and should be prepared apart. (12.2)
30. "Why Forma and Rust rather than Next.js, Bun or Deno?" Pages render on the server in Rust from compiled modules, with no JavaScript engine, virtual DOM or per-request heap there, and a new app is data, a database row, rendered by components compiled once, so adding an app adds no runtime. The spike measures the render rate and memory per render; until it runs, claim the architecture, not a multiple (platform 5, D1).

## Gaps to own before anyone finds them

- The record is long for its level: the build is 64 pages, 56 body and 8 appendix, against a 40-page body budget and 6 for the appendix; both were accepted at the gate with the reason that the remaining words are tables, identifiers and numbers. Own it before a reader counts.
- The cold aggregate over a 1-million-record revision is unmeasured; the 1.8-second deadline and the 100 ms warm operation are assumptions the spike measures. Say "assumed" before anyone asks.
- fAuth's client row for the platform is made by hand, one per environment; the ticket exists and it is not done.
- One founder sits on the release path and the coding agent shares the pipeline; the controls find known advisories and unsigned changes only, and the residual is Medium by the record's own rating.
- There is no customer, no pilot baseline, no pricing and no demand evidence; the seventh goal is marked fuzzy and the first High risk is that this is a well-made platform with no organization.
- The hosting region and the provider tier are assumed for pricing; the $1,150 figure moves with both, the meter does not.
- Two framework tickets, FORMA-232 and FORMA-246, are assumed done and not built; the canvas route has 426 bytes of headroom until they land.
- The record does not say who makes the 20 synthetic rows for a real organization's schema, nor how large the per-organization generation budget is before the stop; both are open, say so rather than improvise.
- The record has no buyer, no price, no hiring plan and no competition beyond 12.2's five (Retool, Airtable, Glide, Claude artifacts, JSON-to-UI libraries); the edition stays silent on all four by rule. Have one honest sentence ready for each: pricing is deferred, the meter first; the first conversation with a design partner is the demo's purpose; a second pair of hands is what money buys.
- The record rates one-person risk High three times and never says how many hours a week the framework, fAuth and the platform take; the reader will ask.
- The edition is dated 2026-10-23, a day after the record; it is the derivation date, say so if asked.
